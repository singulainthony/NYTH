Digital Legacy: What Happens to Your Online Accounts After You're Gone

Most of us have made a will. Far fewer of us have thought about what happens to the hundreds of photographs, emails, and accounts we have accumulated online. Yet those are often the things family members most want — and least able to reach.

When someone passes away without a digital legacy plan, their family usually hits a wall. Apple will not hand over an Apple ID. Google will not give access to a Gmail account. The photos that were supposed to be shared at the funeral are locked behind a password nobody knows. This guide walks through a simple plan you can set up in an afternoon, most of it for free.

Digital legacy planning is a conversation best had with family, while everything is still accessible.

What Counts as a Digital Legacy?

"Digital legacy" simply means everything of yours that lives online or on a device. For most people in North York, that includes:

  • Photo and video libraries — usually the most treasured and least accessible
  • Email accounts, which quietly act as the master key to everything else
  • Banking and investment logins, plus automatic bill payments
  • Social media — Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn
  • Subscriptions that keep charging the estate, such as streaming services and cloud storage
  • Documents in iCloud Drive, Google Drive, or Dropbox
  • Loyalty points and rewards balances

Why This Matters More Than It Used To

Twenty years ago, a filing cabinet held the important things and a family member could simply open it. Today that cabinet is encrypted, and the companies that own it are legally bound to protect your privacy — even from your own children. That is the right policy in general, but it means that access after death has to be arranged in advance. It does not happen automatically.

Important: In Ontario, being the executor of an estate does not automatically give you access to a deceased person's online accounts. Each company has its own process, and many require proof before they will act.

Step One: Make a Simple Inventory

You do not need to list every website you have ever visited. Start with the accounts that would actually matter to the people left behind. Write these on paper, or in a document you keep somewhere physically secure — not in an email to yourself.

  1. Which email address is the "master" one? (This is usually the recovery address for everything else.)
  2. Where do your photos live — iCloud, Google Photos, a laptop, or a mix?
  3. Which financial accounts have online access?
  4. Which subscriptions bill automatically each month?
  5. Do you have two-factor authentication turned on, and on which device does the code arrive?
Pro Tip: Rather than writing passwords on the list, save them in a password manager and use its emergency-access feature. Apple's built-in Passwords app and 1Password both support naming a trusted person who can request access. That is safer than a paper list, and it stays current automatically.

Step Two: Set Up the Three Tools That Do the Work

Apple, Google, and Meta each provide an official way to name someone who can act on your account. Setting these up takes about ten minutes each and costs nothing.

Apple Legacy Contact (for iCloud photo libraries)

  1. Open Settings and tap your name at the top.
  2. Tap Sign-In & Security, then Legacy Contact.
  3. Tap Add Legacy Contact and choose someone from your contacts.
  4. Share the access key with them — in person, or with a printed copy kept with your will.

Your Legacy Contact cannot read your email or private messages, but they can reach your photos, notes, and other data after the waiting period Apple requires.

Google Inactive Account Manager (for Gmail and Google Photos)

  1. Go to myaccount.google.com and sign in.
  2. Open Data & privacy and find Inactive Account Manager.
  3. Choose how long Google should wait before acting — three months is a common choice.
  4. Add up to ten trusted contacts and choose exactly which data each may download.
  5. Optionally, set your account to be deleted after the download period.

Facebook Legacy Contact

  1. Open Settings & privacy, then Settings.
  2. Under Account ownership and control, choose Memorialization.
  3. Name a legacy contact and decide whether they may download an archive of your posts.

Step Three: What to Keep Out of Your Will

Your will is a public document once it goes through probate, which means anything written in it can become visible to people you never intended. Never put passwords, PINs, or access keys in a will. Instead, put them in a sealed envelope stored with your lawyer, your executor, or a safety deposit box, and reference that envelope in the will without listing its contents.

Naming a trusted person now saves your family months of paperwork later.

Step Four: Talk to Your Family This Week

The technology is the easy part. The harder part is the conversation. A useful way to begin is simply: "If something happened to me, would you know where the family photos are and who to contact about my accounts?" Most people discover they would not — and that discovery is what makes the rest of the plan easy to agree on.

Pick one person as your digital executor, ideally someone comfortable with technology and willing to take on the task. Tell them what you have set up, where the sealed envelope is, and who to call if they get stuck.

Caution: Be very careful about anyone who contacts you offering to "manage your digital estate" for a fee, or who asks for account passwords after a death in the family. Grieving families are targeted by these schemes. The legitimate tools are the free ones listed above, set up in advance by you.

Final Thoughts

A digital legacy plan is an act of care, not a sign of pessimism. Ninety minutes of work now can spare your family months of frustration and paperwork later — and it means the photographs, letters, and memories you have collected actually reach the people you meant them for.

Start with one account today. Set up the Legacy Contact you have been meaning to add, and tell that person you have done it. That single step is worth more than a perfect plan you never get around to making.
